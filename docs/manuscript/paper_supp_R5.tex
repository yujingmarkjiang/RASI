\section{When a contrast penalty is inert in one-shot fitting}
Let $X\in\mathbb R^{N\times p}$ be the factual design, $y\in\mathbb R^N$ the outcomes and $B\in\mathbb R^{M\times N}$ a signed incidence matrix mapping factual rows to active-minus-comparator pairs, so that the contrast design is $D=BX$ and the recorded contrasts are $d=By$. The penalised objective is
\begin{equation}
L_\lambda(\beta)=N^{-1}\|X\beta-y\|^2+\lambda M^{-1}\|BX\beta-By\|^2 .
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[Stationarity]
If $X$ has full column rank and $\hat\beta_0$ is the ordinary least-squares solution, then for every $\lambda>0$, $\hat\beta_\lambda=\hat\beta_0$ if and only if $X^\top B^\top B(X\hat\beta_0-y)=0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The normal equation of $L_\lambda$ is $(X^\top X/N+\lambda X^\top B^\top BX/M)\beta=X^\top y/N+\lambda X^\top B^\top By/M$. Substituting $X^\top(X\hat\beta_0-y)=0$ leaves $\lambda X^\top B^\top B(X\hat\beta_0-y)/M=0$. Positive definiteness of $X^\top X$ gives uniqueness.
\end{proof}
Balanced saturated cells with the same participants and pairing multiplicity at every time satisfy the condition, because the summed residual contrast within each active and comparator cell is zero. Complete pairing alone does not: with comparator values $0$ and $10$ and one active value $2$ paired only with the first comparator, cell means $5$ and $2$ give predicted contrast $-3$ against observed $2$, and the gradient is nonzero. In the primary environment the balanced 21-participant subset satisfies the condition to $10^{-12}$ ms; the full 22-participant sample does not exactly, and the measured one-shot difference is $-0.002$ ms (95\% participant-bootstrap interval $-0.011$ to $0.007$; Supplementary Fig.~S2). Ridge changes the condition: with $\eta=0.001$ the pooled contrast MAE of S1 and S2 were 11.77 and 11.85 ms. This note establishes the baseline against which every loop result is read.

\section{Self-training dynamics in linear--Gaussian world models}
\subsection{Setting}
Real data $(X_r,y_r)$ with $n_r$ rows. At round $k$ the model with coefficients $\beta_k$ generates synthetic rows $(X_s,y_s^{(k)})$ at inputs chosen by the loop (hidden arms, unobserved doses). The round-$(k{+}1)$ coefficients minimise
\begin{equation}
\|X_r\beta-y_r\|^2+\|X_s\beta-y_s^{(k)}\|^2+\eta\|\beta\|^2 .
\end{equation}
Write $G_r=X_r^\top X_r$, $G_s=X_s^\top X_s$ and $H=G_r+G_s+\eta I$.

\subsection{Mean rollouts: fixed point and loss of personalisation}
With $y_s^{(k)}=X_s\beta_k$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:map}
\beta_{k+1}=H^{-1}\left(X_r^\top y_r+G_s\beta_k\right).
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[Fixed point on the anchored subspace]
Let $\beta^*$ solve $(G_r+\eta I)\beta^*=X_r^\top y_r$. Then $\beta^*$ is a fixed point of Eq.~\ref{eq:map}; the deviation obeys $\beta_{k+1}-\beta^*=H^{-1}G_s(\beta_k-\beta^*)$; and if round 0 is the real-data ridge fit, $\beta_k=\beta^*$ for every $k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$H^{-1}(X_r^\top y_r+G_s\beta^*)=H^{-1}((G_r+\eta I)\beta^*+G_s\beta^*)=\beta^*$. Subtracting the two normal equations gives the deviation recursion.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}[Spectral structure]
$H^{-1}G_s$ has eigenvalues in $[0,1]$. On eigenvectors of $G_r$ with positive eigenvalue (directions real data inform) the map contracts. On the null space of $G_r$ (directions only synthetic data inform) the eigenvalue is $g/(g+\eta)$, where $g$ is the corresponding eigenvalue of $G_s$: exactly one when $\eta=0$ and strictly below one when $\eta>0$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{remark}[The benign case]
Coordinates in the null space of $G_r$ are participant-by-treatment deviations for hidden arms, or function values at an unobserved dose. With deterministic mean rollouts and any regularisation they decay geometrically toward zero, so the hidden arm's prediction reverts to the population effect that visible participants anchor. Mean-rollout self-training cannot create a wrong contrast in a linear model; it erases personalisation. Collapse requires one of the three ingredients below. Numerically (40-line check, seed 0): with an intercept in the design, the anchored coefficient after 10 mean-rollout rounds differed from the real-data ridge fit by $6.8\times10^{-14}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Stochastic rollouts: the unanchored walk}
Let $\theta_k$ be a null-space coordinate and let round $k$'s $m$ synthetic rows at that coordinate carry $y_s=\theta_k+\epsilon$, $\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. With $c=g/(g+\eta)$, replacement gives $\theta_{k+1}=c(\theta_k+\xi_k)$, $\xi_k=\bar\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2/m)$.
\begin{proposition}[Unanchored walk]
(i) Replacement, $\eta=0$: $\Var(\theta_k)=k\sigma^2/m$ and $\Ex|\theta_k-\theta_{\mathrm{true}}|$ is non-decreasing in $k$. (ii) Replacement, $\eta>0$: $\theta_k$ is a stationary AR(1) with mean 0 and variance $c^2\sigma^2/\{m(1-c^2)\}$. (iii) Accumulation: $\theta_k\to\theta_1$ with $\Var(\theta_k-\theta_1)<0.65\,\sigma^2/m$; the error neither grows nor returns.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) sums independent increments; (ii) is the AR(1) stationary variance $c^2\Var(\xi)/(1-c^2)$; (iii) follows from the running mean of independent draws, $\sum_{j\ge2}j^{-2}<0.65$.
\end{proof}
Numerical check ($\sigma=8$, $m=5$, 4,000 replicates): observed variance at $k=10,20,30$ was 128.2, 264.6 and 398.1 against 128, 256 and 384 predicted; accumulation plateaued at 20.5.

\subsection{A plausibility verifier biases the walk}
If each rollout $\theta_k+\epsilon$ is retained with probability $w(\theta_k+\epsilon)$ and retained rollouts are averaged,
\begin{equation}
\Ex[\xi_k\mid\theta_k]=\Cov(\epsilon,w)/\Ex[w] .
\end{equation}
\begin{proposition}[Verifier drift]
For a Gaussian-likelihood filter $w(x)=\exp\{-(x-\mu)^2/2s^2\}$ centred on the pooled trajectory $\mu$, the retained-rollout mean is $(s^2\theta_k+\sigma^2\mu)/(s^2+\sigma^2)$, so the increment has mean $-\sigma^2(\theta_k-\mu)/(s^2+\sigma^2)$. A treatment arm whose true response differs from the pooled trajectory drifts toward the null contrast at a rate set by the filter's selectivity $s$.
\end{proposition}
This is the mechanism by which self-verification, which prevents factual collapse\cite{fu2025selfverification}, causes contrast collapse: the verifier is blind to treatment and rewards typicality.

\subsection{A contrast anchor bounds the walk}
\begin{proposition}[AR(1) anchor]
With an added penalty $\lambda(\theta-a)^2$ toward a real-data value $a$, $\theta_{k+1}=(\lambda a+\theta_k+\xi_k)/(1+\lambda)$ is a stationary AR(1) with mean $a$ and variance $\sigma^2/\{m\lambda(2+\lambda)\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $\phi=1/(1+\lambda)$, $\theta_{k+1}-a=\phi(\theta_k-a)+\phi\xi_k$, whose stationary variance is $\phi^2\sigma^2/\{m(1-\phi^2)\}=\sigma^2/\{m\lambda(2+\lambda)\}$.
\end{proof}
Numerical check: at $\lambda=0.1,0.3,1,3$ the observed stationary variances were 60.5, 19.5, 4.32 and 0.84 against 60.9, 18.6, 4.27 and 0.85.

\subsection{The audit gate as a reflected walk}
Let $A_k$ be held-out visible-pair contrast error and $H_k$ hidden-arm error after round $k$. The gate accepts round $k$ only if $A_k-A_{k-1}\le\tau$. If increments satisfy $\Delta A=\rho_{AH}\Delta H+\nu$ with independent noise $\nu$, an accepted round has $\Ex[\Delta H\mid\text{accept}]\le(\tau-\Ex[\nu\mid\text{accept}])/\rho_{AH}$, so expected hidden drift over $K$ rounds is at most $K\tau/\rho_{AH}$ plus a noise term. Gate efficiency therefore depends on $\rho_{AH}$, which is reported for every architecture and scale (Supplementary Tables~S7 and S15).

\section{Partial anchoring and who collapses first}
\begin{proposition}[Partial anchoring]
Let a subgroup's treatment coordinate $\theta_g$ be informed by $n_g$ real observations under that treatment, with sample mean $\bar y_g$, and by $n_s$ synthetic rollouts per round from the current model, with per-rollout variance $\sigma^2$. Under replacement and $\eta=0$,
\begin{equation}
\theta_{g,k+1}-\bar y_g=\varphi(\theta_{g,k}-\bar y_g)+\varphi\bar\epsilon_k,\qquad \varphi=\frac{n_s}{n_g+n_s},\qquad \Var_\infty(\theta_g)=\frac{\varphi^2}{1-\varphi^2}\,\frac{\sigma^2}{n_s}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The least-squares estimate is the weighted mean $(n_g\bar y_g+n_s(\theta_{g,k}+\bar\epsilon_k))/(n_g+n_s)$; subtract $\bar y_g$.
\end{proof}
The proposition unifies Propositions 2 and 4: $n_g=0$ gives $\varphi=1$ and the unanchored walk; $n_g>0$ is an anchor with $\lambda=n_g/n_s$. Its clinical reading is direct. A group that less often receives a treatment has small $n_g$ for that treatment and many untreated members whose treated arm is rolled out (large $n_s$), so $\varphi$ is close to one and its counterfactual arm under that treatment is the least anchored in the model. If instead real and synthetic counts both scale with the group's overall share $\pi$, $\varphi$ is unchanged and $\Var_\infty\propto1/\pi$, so the walk standard deviation, and to first order $\CCI_K$, scale as $\pi^{-1/2}$. Both predictions are tested in E4 (Supplementary Table~S24; Supplementary Fig.~S11). With ridge $\eta>0$ the small-group coordinate is also shrunk toward the pooled value, trading walk variance for bias toward the population effect, which is itself a subgroup error when effects differ.

\section{Why fresh factual data and accumulation do not help}
\begin{proposition}[Fresh factual data]
Let new real rows $X_{\mathrm{new}}$ be added each round. If every row of $X_{\mathrm{new}}$ lies in the row space of $X_r$, then $\mathrm{null}(G_r+X_{\mathrm{new}}^\top X_{\mathrm{new}})=\mathrm{null}(G_r)$, and the dynamics of Propositions 2.2--2.4 on that null space are unchanged.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For positive semidefinite $G_r$ and $X_{\mathrm{new}}^\top X_{\mathrm{new}}$, $v^\top(G_r+X_{\mathrm{new}}^\top X_{\mathrm{new}})v=0$ if and only if $X_rv=0$ and $X_{\mathrm{new}}v=0$; the second is implied by the first when the rows of $X_{\mathrm{new}}$ lie in the row space of $X_r$.
\end{proof}
In model collapse of generative models, real data span the support of the distribution, so fresh or accumulated real data re-anchor every direction\cite{shumailov2024,gerstgrasser2024}. In a world model, new patients observed under the treatments they actually received add rows in the factual row space. They improve factual error and the anchored coordinates but leave the counterfactual arms exactly as unanchored as before. Only data that observe the previously unobserved arm, for example crossover periods, randomised assignment to the other arm or a published randomised contrast used as an anchor, change the null space. Accumulation of synthetic data, which prevents generative collapse, freezes the counterfactual coordinate at its first stochastic draw (Proposition 2.2(iii)); it stops further drift but does not undo the first round's error.

\section{Rollout temperature, horizon and compounding}
\textbf{Temperature.} For a Gaussian predictive head sampled at temperature $T$, the rollout standard deviation is $T\sigma$, so Proposition 2.2 holds with $\sigma^2$ replaced by $T^2\sigma^2$: $\Var(\theta_k)=kT^2\sigma^2/m$. Temperature zero recovers mean rollouts and the benign case; the collapse index under replacement is predicted to scale linearly in $T$ at fixed $k$. For categorical heads (event transformers, language models) temperature changes the entropy of the sampled sequence rather than a variance; the prediction is monotone but not linear.

\textbf{Horizon.} For autoregressive rollouts of horizon $h$ with linearised one-step dynamics $y_{t+1}=\rho y_t+\epsilon_t$, the variance of the $h$-step rollout around its mean is $\sigma^2(1-\rho^{2h})/(1-\rho^2)$, which increases with $h$. Longer free-running rollouts therefore enter the walk with a larger effective $\sigma^2$ and collapse faster; any one-step bias also compounds. The horizon ablation tests the predicted ordering (Supplementary Table~S17).

\textbf{Synthetic-to-real ratio.} On the null space the ratio does not enter (Proposition 2.2). On partially anchored coordinates it enters through $\varphi=n_s/(n_g+n_s)$ (Note 3), predicting a saturating increase of $\CCI_K$ with the ratio.

\section{Uncertainty-weighted rollouts and ensembles}
\textbf{Uncertainty weighting converts collapse into shrinkage.} Suppose synthetic rows are down-weighted by $w\in(0,1]$ according to the model's epistemic uncertainty, which is largest on unanchored arms. On the null space with ridge $\eta$, $\theta_{k+1}=c_w(\theta_k+\xi_k)$ with $c_w=wg/(wg+\eta)<c_1$. The walk becomes an AR(1) around zero with smaller variance $c_w^2\sigma^2/\{m(1-c_w^2)\}$, but the coordinate is pulled toward the prior, which for a participant deviation is the population effect and for an unconstrained contrast is the null. Uncertainty weighting therefore reduces walk variance and increases bias toward the null; it does not supply the missing anchor (Supplementary Table~S21; Supplementary Fig.~S8).

\textbf{Ensemble disagreement detects the walk but not verifier drift.} Let ensemble member $j$ carry $\theta^{(j)}_k=\theta_0+D_k+W^{(j)}_k$, where $D_k$ is a deterministic drift common to all members (from a shared verifier) and $W^{(j)}_k$ are independent walks. Disagreement $\Var_j(\theta^{(j)}_k)=\Var(W_k)=k\sigma^2/m$ grows with $k$ under the walk and is independent of $D_k$. Ensemble disagreement\cite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} is therefore a label-free detector of stochastic collapse and is blind to verifier-driven collapse; negative-control drift and the contrast audit see both (Supplementary Table~S23).

\section{Neural world models and effective regularisation}
Gradient descent on least squares, stopped after $T$ steps at learning rate $\gamma$, behaves in each eigendirection of the design approximately as ridge with $\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx(\gamma T)^{-1}$\cite{ali2019earlystopping}. Early stopping on visible factual loss applies an effective penalty tuned to the anchored subspace and uninformed about the unanchored one. With Proposition 2.2 this predicts that neural world models trained with factual early stopping (i) revert hidden-arm personalisation toward the population effect under mean rollouts, (ii) follow an AR(1) walk with shrinkage set by $\eta_{\mathrm{eff}}$ under stochastic rollouts and (iii) show no signature of either in validation curves. For nonlinear networks the null space of $G_r$ is replaced by input regions whose tangent features are nearly orthogonal to those of the real data; the predictions keep their sign but not their constants. Re-initialising each round rather than warm-starting removes the dependence on $\theta_k$ only if the synthetic data do not carry it, which they do; the ablation tests whether re-initialisation changes $\CCI_K$ (Supplementary Table~S22). Treatment decodability from the latent state (linear-probe AUROC) and centred kernel alignment between round-0 and round-$k$ representations\cite{kornblith2019cka} are reported to test whether collapse is accompanied by erosion of treatment information in the latent (Supplementary Table~S26).

\section{Measurement floor, collapse index and equivalence}
\subsection{Measurement floor}
Within each training fold, replicate variance $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rep}}$ is pooled across participant--period--time cells with at least two recordings, weighted by degrees of freedom. For replicate counts $n_{at},n_{a0},n_{0t},n_{00}$ in the four cells of the contrast, the measurement variance is $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{rep}}(n_{at}^{-1}+n_{a0}^{-1}+n_{0t}^{-1}+n_{00}^{-1})$, and $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ times its square root is the expected absolute error under independent Gaussian noise. In the primary environment this is 5.42 ms, 18.5\% of the recorded contrast SD. It is a technical component only: period-to-period biological variation and covariance among the four means are not identified by one exposure per drug\cite{senn2002crossover}.
\subsection{Inference for $\CCI_k$}
$\CCI_k=(C_k-C_0)/\phi$ is a paired difference on the same hidden units divided by a fixed scale. Within a mask, participants are bootstrapped with multiplicity retained (2,000 replicates). Across masks and seeds we report the median and 2.5--97.5 percentiles of within-run estimates and fit $\CCI_{k,r}=\alpha_r+\beta_r k+e_{k,r}$ with random intercepts and slopes for mask and seed; the fixed slope is the collapse rate per round. ``$\CCI_K\ge1$ in most masks'' uses the proportion of masks whose within-mask lower 95\% bound exceeds one, with a Wilson interval.
\subsection{Factual equivalence}
Decoupling is tested by two one-sided tests\cite{schuirmann1987tost} of $H_0:|\Delta F_K|\ge\delta$ with $\delta=1$ ms in E1 and 1\% of $F_0$ elsewhere; equivalence is declared when both one-sided $p<0.05$. A factual change that is statistically equivalent to zero while $\CCI_K\ge1$ is the operational definition of counterfactual collapse used throughout.

\section{The randomised-anchor audit and misspecified anchors}
\subsection{Definition}
A randomised trial reports $\hat\Delta_{\mathrm{RCT}}$ with standard error $s$ for population $P$. For a cohort with baseline variables $V$, the trial-eligible subpopulation is $E=\{i:V_i\text{ satisfies the trial's criteria}\}$. The model's predicted contrast on $E$ is $\hat\Delta_{\mathrm{model}}(E)$ with bootstrap standard error $\hat s$, and $z=(\hat\Delta_{\mathrm{model}}(E)-\hat\Delta_{\mathrm{RCT}})/\sqrt{s^2+\hat s^2}$. Equivalence uses two one-sided tests at the trial's 95\% half-width.
\subsection{Transportability}
The audit tests the model if $\Ex[\tau_i\mid i\in E]=\Ex[\tau_i\mid i\in P]$, which holds when effect modifiers are distributed alike in $E$ and $P$ or the effect is homogeneous in them\cite{pearl2014transport,dahabreh2020transport}. We check this partially by comparing the model's subgroup contrasts on $E$ with the trial's reported subgroup estimates; a model that matches the overall anchor but inverts the trial's subgroup ordering fails. Comparisons of emulated and randomised estimates suggest that agreement is achievable but not automatic\cite{rctduplicate2023}.
\subsection{A wrong anchor can still be better than none}
If the anchor $a$ is biased by $b$ relative to the true contrast, Proposition 2.4 gives stationary mean squared error $b^2+\sigma^2/\{m\lambda(2+\lambda)\}$. Without an anchor, replacement gives $K\sigma^2/m$ at round $K$. The anchor is preferable whenever
\begin{equation}
|b|<b^*=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{m}\left(K-\frac{1}{\lambda(2+\lambda)}\right)} ,
\end{equation}
which grows with the number of rounds. The anchor-misspecification ablation biases the anchor by $\pm0.5$, $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ trial standard errors and tests this threshold (Supplementary Table~S20; Supplementary Fig.~S7).

\section{Negative-control interventions}
Let $N_i(t)$ be an action with no plausible effect on $Y$ over the horizon. A world model given $N$ as a treatment input implies a contrast $\hat\tau^N_i=\hat Y_i(N=1)-\hat Y_i(N=0)$ whose true value is zero\cite{lipsitch2010}. At round 0, $\hat\tau^N$ measures residual confounding and overfitting; its change across rounds measures unanchored variance directly, because the $N=1$ arm is constrained only by its coincidental co-occurrence with the outcome. Under Proposition 2.2 the negative-control contrast walks with the same per-round variance as any hidden arm, so its variance across seeds estimates $k\sigma^2/m$ without counterfactual truth. It detects spurious effects, not missed real ones, which is why E4, E5 and E7 also inject known effects.

\section{Treatment signal and the factual--contrast bound}
With $m_a(x)=\Ex[Y\mid X=x,A=a]$ and $m(x)=\Ex[Y\mid X=x]$, the law of total variance gives $R^*_{\mathrm{blind}}-R^*_{\mathrm{aware}}=\Ex\{\Var[m_A(X)\mid X]\}$, and for binary treatment with propensity in $[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$ squared error in the conditional-mean contrast is bounded by excess factual risk divided by $\epsilon(1-\epsilon)$. Under self-training the bound is uninformative on the unanchored subspace, because excess factual risk is undefined where there are no factual data. This is the formal reason why factual benchmarks cannot detect counterfactual collapse.


\section{Precision, power and cohort size}
The precision of a collapse estimate is set by the number of patients whose hidden arms are scored. Resampling the 87 recorded participant--drug contrasts at two hours gives the cross-participant residual standard deviation of the saturated model, 16.2 ms, and hence an expected hidden-arm error of 12.9 ms at round 0 for a model with no participant-specific information. Drawing participants with replacement to a target cohort size, taking 30\% of their arms as hidden and bootstrapping participants within each draw gives the standard error of the hidden-arm mean absolute error as a function of cohort size (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s32}; Supplementary Fig.~S19). It falls as $n^{-1/2}$: 2.10 ms at 22 participants, 1.30 ms at 60 and 1.07 ms at 90. In floor units the smallest collapse detectable at 80\% power in a single mask is 1.08, 0.67 and 0.55; across 50 masks, whose errors are correlated through the shared cohort, the median over masks is the reported statistic and its across-mask interval is the reported uncertainty.

Three consequences follow for cohort planning. First, a cohort of 60 to 90 patients is sufficient to detect a collapse of about half a measurement floor in a single mask, which is the magnitude predicted by Proposition 2.2 after five rounds at $m=5$. Second, subgroup analyses require cohort size rather than mask count: a subgroup holding a quarter of a 90-patient cohort is as large as the whole 22-participant primary environment, so the partial-anchoring prediction of Supplementary Note 3 becomes estimable within a cohort rather than only across simulated shares. Third, the number of treatments per patient matters as much as the number of patients: hidden arms, not patients, are the unit of the estimate, so a cohort of 60 patients with four arms each yields more precision than 90 patients with two.

\section{Retrieval-anchored rollouts and distillation}
\textbf{Retrieval.} Let the rollout for an unobserved arm be the recorded trajectory of the $k$ nearest real patients who did receive that arm, averaged, rather than a sample from the model. The synthetic row then carries the real value $\bar y_{\mathcal N}$ plus sampling noise $\sigma^2_{\mathrm{real}}/k$, and does not carry $\theta_k$. The round update becomes
\begin{equation}
\theta_{k+1}=\frac{n_g\bar y_g+n_s\bar y_{\mathcal N}}{n_g+n_s}+\xi_k,\qquad \Var(\xi_k)=\frac{n_s^2}{(n_g+n_s)^2}\frac{\sigma^2_{\mathrm{real}}}{k},
\end{equation}
which has no dependence on the previous round: the walk is replaced by independent draws around a real-data value, and the error is bounded by the bias of the neighbourhood, $|\bar y_{\mathcal N}-\theta_{\mathrm{true}}|$, rather than growing with $k$. Retrieval is therefore an anchor obtained through the rollout mechanism rather than through the loss, and its risk is neighbourhood bias when the nearest patients differ systematically from the target. The prediction registered in H21 is that retrieval at least halves $\CCI_K$; the ablation also reports the neighbourhood size $k$ and the resulting bias.

\textbf{Distillation.} A student trained once on a teacher's rollouts inherits the teacher's counterfactual error without adding a new walk, because the teacher is fixed: a single generation is a bias, not a drift. Recursive distillation, in which each student becomes the next teacher, is exactly the replacement regime of Proposition 2.2 with $m$ equal to the number of distillation samples, and its variance grows linearly in generations. The ablation separates these two cases, which are often described by the same word in practice.

\section{A reused audit can be overfitted}
The gate's audit statistic is an estimate on a finite set of real pairs. If the same set is scored at every round, and the model or agent is selected on it, the audit becomes an adaptively reused holdout. For an audit set of $n_a$ pairs, the audit statistic has standard error $s_a\approx\sigma_c/\sqrt{n_a}$, and after $R$ selection steps the expected optimistic bias of the best-scoring candidate grows approximately as $s_a\sqrt{2\log R}$. The hidden drift that this bias can conceal is that bias divided by the gate efficiency, so
\begin{equation}
\text{concealable drift}\;\approx\;\frac{\sigma_c}{\rho_{AH}}\sqrt{\frac{2\log R}{n_a}} .
\end{equation}
Three designs follow. A fixed audit set is safe only when $n_a\gg 2\log R/\varepsilon^2$ for the tolerated drift $\varepsilon$. A rotated audit set, a fresh subset drawn each round from a reserved pool, replaces $R$ by the number of reuses of any one pair. A held-out audit set, scored once at the end, has no adaptive bias at all but gives no feedback during the loop. The experiment in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s39} measures all three against an agent that sees the audit score, and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s39b} measures the audit-set size needed to hold $\CCI_K$ below 0.4 at fixed $\rho_{AH}$. This is the quantitative version of a simple rule: the contrast data used to audit a self-improving system must be reserved from it, exactly as a test set is reserved from training.

\section{Federated self-training across sites}
Let $S$ sites each run the loop on their own patients and average their model parameters each round, as in federated averaging. For an unanchored coordinate, site $s$ contributes $\theta^{(s)}_k=\theta_0+D_k+W^{(s)}_k$, where $D_k$ is drift common to all sites, for example from a shared verifier or a shared plausibility prior, and $W^{(s)}_k$ are site-specific walks. The averaged coordinate is $\theta_0+D_k+\bar W_k$ with $\Var(\bar W_k)=k\sigma^2/(mS)$.
\begin{proposition}[Federated averaging]
Federated averaging reduces the variance of stochastic counterfactual collapse by a factor $S$ and leaves common drift unchanged. Expected absolute hidden-arm error therefore falls as $S^{-1/2}$ under the stochastic mechanism and not at all under the verifier mechanism.
\end{proposition}
The practical reading is uncomfortable: federation makes the harmless-looking mechanism look even more harmless while leaving the mechanism that moves contrasts toward the null untouched, and it does so while making the collapse harder to see at any single site. A federated system therefore needs a contrast audit at the aggregation step, not only at each site. Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s36} tests the $S^{-1/2}$ prediction at one, three and six sites for both mechanisms.


\section{The method and its safe-improvement guarantee}
\textbf{Algorithm.} Inputs: real data $\mathcal D$; a reserved pool of real paired contrasts $\mathcal A$; a base model $f_0$ trained on $\mathcal D$ alone; acceptance margin $\varepsilon$; rounds $R$.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
\item \emph{Allocate.} Train a five-member ensemble on the current data. For each unobserved arm $j$, compute the ensemble's disagreement $v_j$ about its contrast and allocate $m_j\propto v_j$ rollouts, so synthetic data go where the model is least determined rather than uniformly.
\item \emph{Propose rollouts.} For arm $j$, take the recorded trajectories of the $k$ nearest real patients who received that treatment, in the current model's representation, and average them. Discard any candidate whose implied contrast differs from the anchor by more than three anchor standard errors.
\item \emph{Retrain.} Fit $f_{r+1}$ on real plus accepted synthetic data with the contrast penalty of weight $\lambda$ on visible recorded pairs.
\item \emph{Audit.} Draw a fresh audit subset $A_r\subset\mathcal A$ and compute the contrast error $\hat C(f_{r+1};A_r)$.
\item \emph{Accept or revert.} Keep $f_{r+1}$ if $\hat C(f_{r+1};A_r)\le\hat C(f_r;A_r)-\varepsilon$; otherwise restore $f_r$ and resample the allocation. Stop after ten consecutive rejections.
\end{enumerate}
The base model $f_0$ is the model a developer would otherwise ship, so every gain is reported against it rather than against a weakened control.

\textbf{Guarantee.} Write $C(f)$ for the true contrast error and $\hat C(f;A)$ for its estimate on an audit set of $n_a$ independent pairs with per-pair error sub-Gaussian with parameter $\sigma_c$.
\begin{proposition}[Safe improvement]
Let $f_R$ be the model after $R$ rounds of the algorithm with margin $\varepsilon$, audit sets drawn independently from the reserved pool, and let $\delta\in(0,1)$. Then with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\begin{equation}
C(f_R)\;\le\;C(f_0)-\Big(\varepsilon-2\sigma_c\sqrt{\tfrac{\log(2R/\delta)}{n_a}}\Big)\,\cdot\,\#\{\text{accepted rounds}\},
\end{equation}
so that whenever $\varepsilon>2\sigma_c\sqrt{\log(2R/\delta)/n_a}$ no accepted model is worse than the base model, and each acceptance is a real improvement of at least the bracketed amount.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A union bound over the $2R$ audit evaluations gives $|\hat C(f;A_r)-C(f)|\le\sigma_c\sqrt{\log(2R/\delta)/n_a}$ simultaneously, by Hoeffding's inequality for sub-Gaussian averages. On that event an accepted round satisfies $C(f_{r+1})\le\hat C(f_{r+1};A_r)+\sigma_c\sqrt{\cdot}\le\hat C(f_r;A_r)-\varepsilon+\sigma_c\sqrt{\cdot}\le C(f_r)-\varepsilon+2\sigma_c\sqrt{\cdot}$, and a rejected round leaves the model unchanged. Telescoping over accepted rounds gives the claim.
\end{proof}
Three remarks. The bound degrades as $\sqrt{\log R}$, so a long loop needs a modestly larger audit rather than a proportionally larger one. It requires the audit sets to be drawn from a pool reserved from training, which is why rotation matters (Supplementary Note 14). And it bounds the contrast error, not the factual error, so the factual-equivalence test is reported alongside it rather than implied by it.

\section{Where the improvement comes from}
A loop that only avoided harm would end at the base model. The method ends above it through three identifiable routes, separated by the component ablation in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:s44}.

\emph{Shared structure.} Synthetic rows at unobserved arms still constrain parameters shared with observed arms, such as time profiles and dose--response shape. Where those are poorly determined by real data alone, rollouts act as regularisation and reduce variance in the contrast, which is the only route available to the naive loop and the reason its gain is positive for a few rounds before the walk overtakes it.

\emph{Imported real information.} Retrieval replaces a sample from the model's belief with recorded trajectories of similar patients who did receive the treatment. This adds information that was in the data but outside the model's fitted support, so the contrast error can fall below its round-0 value rather than merely stop rising (Supplementary Note 13).

\emph{Directed search.} Allocating rollouts by ensemble disagreement spends the synthetic budget on the arms whose contrasts are least determined, and the acceptance rule keeps only the proposals that pay off on a held-out audit. With an agent in the proposal step the same mechanism searches over architectures and regularisers instead of over rollout allocations.

The decomposition is testable: removing retrieval should remove most of the gain above zero while leaving the loop safe, and removing the acceptance rule should leave the gain in expectation but reintroduce the risk of ending below the base model. Both are registered in H25.

\section{Intuitive demonstrations and sanity tests}
These are small, checkable demonstrations a reader can reproduce in minutes; they carry no clinical claim and exist so that the mechanism and the method can be seen directly.

\textbf{D1, one unanchored number} (Supplementary Fig.~S25). A single scalar whose true value is 1 is re-estimated for 20 rounds from $m=5$ noisy self-generated draws. Under the naive loop it random-walks away; under a plausibility filter it is pulled to zero; under the anchor it stays; under retrieval it is redrawn around the truth each round; under the full method, which accepts a step only when it improves, the error falls. The panel reports the mean absolute error at the first and last round for each.

\textbf{D2, two real participants} (Supplementary Fig.~S26). The recorded two-hour ranolazine contrasts of the strongest and weakest responder are taken as ground truth, and the loop is run at the measured rollout noise. The naive loop drifts across the 10 ms line in both directions; the anchored loop converges on the recorded value. This is the clinical form of the statistic: the same patient classified differently by a model whose factual accuracy has not changed.

\textbf{D3, placebo against placebo.} Both arms of a contrast are drawn from placebo periods, so the true effect is exactly zero. Any non-zero contrast is model artefact, and its growth across rounds is the walk with no signal present.

\textbf{D4, give the model the answer.} The hidden arms are revealed during training. The contrast error must fall to the measurement floor and stay there; if it does not, the deficit is optimisation or capacity, not anchoring, and the collapse measurement is confounded.

\textbf{D5, permuted treatment labels.} Treatment labels are permuted within participant, destroying the effect while preserving every marginal. The loop should show no collapse beyond the walk's variance, and any systematic drift indicates leakage of outcome information into the treatment channel.

\textbf{D6, oracle gate.} The gate is given the true hidden error instead of the audit estimate. The gap between the oracle gate and the real gate is the price of estimating the audit, and it should close as the audit set grows, which is the empirical counterpart of Supplementary Note 16.
